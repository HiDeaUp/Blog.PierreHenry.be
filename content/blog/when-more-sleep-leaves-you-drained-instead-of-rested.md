**Personal productivity and sleep**

This morning, I woke up groggy and out of sorts. My Whoop sleep tracker—usually strapped to my ankle—showed I’d slept a full nine hours. On paper, that sounds ideal. But instead of feeling refreshed, I was weighed down by guilt and a sense of wasted time. Was I simply oversleeping, or was my body signaling something deeper?

I’ve tracked my sleep for a while now, experimenting with devices and routines. The Whoop bracelet has become part of my daily ritual, even if its battery life and lack of airplane mode can be frustrating. Still, checking my stats and reflecting on my rest helps me notice patterns in my energy and motivation.

Today’s sluggishness reminded me: both too little and too much sleep can throw me off. There’s a balance to strike, and finding it is more personal than prescriptive.

### When More Sleep Backfires

It’s easy to assume more sleep is always better, but oversleeping can leave me just as drained as a short night. After nine hours, I didn’t leap out of bed—I lingered, frustrated that my morning plans had slipped away. That familiar self-criticism crept in: I’d “wasted” time, missed opportunities, and lost momentum.

But the impact goes beyond lost hours. Oversleeping can sap motivation and feed inertia. I’ve noticed this happens most when I lack clear projects or goals. After finishing a big project or in between milestones, my sense of direction fades, and even getting up feels like a challenge.

### Motivation Needs a Target

My motivation is strongest when I have meaningful, achievable projects—not endless marathons, but medium-sized goals I can finish in a week or two. Breaking big ambitions into smaller, concrete steps makes it easier to start each day with purpose.

There’s real satisfaction in completing things. That’s why I focus on projects with clear endpoints, so I can regularly experience that sense of achievement. I discuss this in my book, “Le succès n’est pas loin,” which I’ve updated often since 2020. (If you read it, I’d love your feedback—especially if you make it past the first few pages!)

### Finding Your Sleep Sweet Spot

So, how much sleep is enough? For me, six to seven hours is ideal. More than that, and I feel lethargic and unfocused. Less, and I’m running on empty. Everyone’s “just right” is different, so it’s important to listen to your body and notice how you feel after different amounts of rest.

Sleep isn’t a simple equation. Too little creates a deficit; too much, and you feel overdosed. The challenge is to find your own equilibrium—where you wake up energized and ready to tackle the day.

### Start Your Day by “Eating the Frog”

One habit that’s changed my productivity: tackling my hardest task first thing in the morning. Brian Tracy’s “Eat That Frog” analogy sums it up—if you have to eat a frog and a piece of chocolate, eat the frog first. The “frog” is your most difficult or unpleasant task; the “chocolate” is your reward.

If you start with the easy stuff, you’ll keep putting off the hard task, letting it grow in your mind. But if you handle the tough job right away, you clear mental space and set a positive tone for the rest of the day. I try to do this before checking emails or messages, when my energy and focus are highest. That way, no matter what else happens, I’ve accomplished something meaningful.

### Beware the Trap of Overconsumption

As a data science student, I have access to a huge digital library. It’s tempting to download more books than I’ll ever read—like piling your plate too high at a buffet. This over-preparation is often disguised procrastination. We collect resources, thinking more options will make us more productive, but it usually leads to overwhelm and indecision. The solution: only take what you truly need, and use it well.

### Perseverance, Not Stubbornness

Motivation and perseverance matter, but so does knowing when to pivot. I’m reminded of a YouTuber who nearly quit her channel, then found new energy after reading Seth Godin’s “The Dip.” Her story shows the power of persistence—but also the importance of changing course when something isn’t working. Sometimes, the bravest move is to iterate and try a new approach.

### Practical Steps for Better Sleep and Productivity

Here’s what’s helped me find my rhythm:

1. **Track your sleep**: Notice what amount leaves you feeling your best.
2. **Set achievable projects**: Break big goals into smaller, time-bound tasks.
3. **Tackle the hardest task first**: Use your morning energy to “eat the frog.”
4. **Limit resource hoarding**: Only download or buy what you’ll actually use.
5. **Reflect and adjust**: Notice motivation dips, and don’t hesitate to change your approach.

Ultimately, the goal isn’t just productivity—it’s happiness. That means investing in yourself, taking action, and learning by doing. Don’t put off what matters most. Do it today, and give your time—and your sleep—the respect they deserve.
