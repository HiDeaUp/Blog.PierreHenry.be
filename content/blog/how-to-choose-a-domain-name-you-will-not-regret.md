A domain name becomes part of the product. It appears in links, email addresses, conversations, invoices, and printed material. I want a name that still works if the product grows beyond its first feature.

I no longer treat `.com` as a requirement. The audience, the words, and the ability to keep the domain secure matter more.

## I Test the Name Out Loud

I say the candidate name without displaying it and ask someone to type what they heard. This catches problems that a logo can hide.

I also check whether the name:

1. depends on an unusual spelling;
2. contains an ambiguous number;
3. remains readable in lowercase;
4. limits the product to a temporary feature;
5. sounds too similar to an established service;
6. creates an unfortunate phrase when the words join together.

A hyphen can make some names easier to read, but it also needs to be explained in speech. I do not ban it. I ask whether the clarity is worth the extra instruction.

## The Extension Should Fit the Audience

A country-code extension such as `.fr`, `.be`, `.ch`, or `.ca` can make a geographic focus clear. A generic extension may fit a product that is not tied to one country.

Google says it treats new generic top-level domains like other gTLDs. A keyword in the extension does not create a ranking advantage. Country-code extensions mainly provide a geographic signal. The current [Google SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) explains this without the old rule that every serious site needs a `.com`.

I choose the extension for people and for the expected life of the product, not for an SEO promise.

## Availability Is Only the First Check

A domain being available does not mean the name is free of conflicts. I search the web, app stores, and the social platforms used by the audience. I look for products with similar names, spellings, or pronunciation.

I also check trademark databases. The [WIPO Global Brand Database](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/global-brand-database) is a useful starting point, but WIPO advises checking relevant national and regional registers too. When the name carries real business value, a free search does not replace legal advice.

This step is less exciting than naming ideas, but changing a domain after launch affects email, links, search visibility, documentation, and user trust.

## I Compare Renewal and Recovery

The lowest first-year price is not enough. I compare:

- renewal and restoration costs;
- multi-factor authentication;
- registrar lock controls;
- DNS and DNSSEC support;
- the transfer process;
- support quality;
- ownership records and account recovery.

For a generic domain, I confirm that the provider appears in the [ICANN list of accredited registrars](https://www.icann.org/en/contracted-parties/accredited-registrars/list-of-accredited-registrars), or I understand which accredited registrar sits behind the reseller.

Accreditation does not tell me which interface or support team is best. It tells me who has the direct contractual role for the registration.

## I Secure the Domain Immediately

I enable automatic renewal, payment alerts, multi-factor authentication, and the registrar lock. I avoid using only an address on the same domain as the recovery email. If the domain fails, I still need a route back into the account.

I record who owns the domain, where it is registered, when it renews, and how another trusted person can recover it. A company domain should not live only inside one employee's personal account.

If I register several extensions to reduce confusion, I choose one canonical address and permanently redirect the others. Google also recommends a consistent canonical URL instead of serving the same content from several domains.

The shortest available name is not automatically the best one. I prefer the domain that users can understand and the team can renew, transfer, and recover without a crisis.
